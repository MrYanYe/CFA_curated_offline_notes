Understanding Basic Earnings Per Share | CFA Level I FSA<\/h1>\n\n\n\n

In this lesson, we’ll explore earnings per share (EPS)<\/b>, its significance, and how to calculate basic EPS<\/b>. So, let’s dive in and get a better understanding of this important financial metric!<\/p>\n\n\n\n

Why Earnings Per Share Matters<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

Imagine owning 10,000 shares in a company, and a year later, you discover the company’s net earnings increased by 20%. Sounds great, right? But then you find out the number of outstanding shares doubled over the past year. This means your share of the earnings has effectively been reduced, as the proportion of the company’s equity you own has been halved.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

A better measure of a company’s profitability is one that takes into account the number of outstanding shares. The earnings per share, or EPS, is one such measure. It is a commonly used corporate profitability performance measure.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

What is Earnings Per Share?<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

EPS is a measure of a company’s profitability, considering the number of outstanding shares. It’s a common performance measure reported for shares of common stock under both IFRS and US GAAP. EPS helps you understand your share of the company’s earnings better.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

Simple vs. Complex Capital Structure<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

A company’s capital structure can be simple or complex, depending on whether it has issued any potentially dilutive securities, like convertible bonds, convertible preferred stock, employee stock options, or warrants:<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    \n
  • Simple capital structure: Contains only common stock, non-convertible debt, and non-convertible preferred stock.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
  • Complex capital structure: Has issued any potentially dilutive securities.<\/li>\n<\/ul>\n\n\n\n

    Companies with simple capital structures report only basic EPS, while firms with complex capital structures must report both basic and diluted EPS.<\/p>\n\n\n\n
