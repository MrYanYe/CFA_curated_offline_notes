    Financial Accounting vs. Tax Reporting<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

    To understand the differences between financial accounting and tax reporting, let’s start with a simple example:<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    A company reports a revenue of $100,000 for the year. Deducting costs of $60,000, the company has a pre-tax income of $40,000. This is also known as the accounting profit.<\/b> Let\u2019s say income is taxed 30%, the company reports a tax expense of $12,000. The net profit is therefore $28,000.<\/b><\/p>\n\n\n\n

    This is known as financial accounting, and what we are familiar with so far.<\/b><\/p>\n\n\n\n

    When it comes to actual income taxes paid, the tax reporting could be different. Under tax reporting, the tax authorities may have different standards when it comes to recognising costs.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    Such differences can be permanent, or temporary. We shall discuss some of these differences later.<\/b><\/p>\n\n\n\n

    Let\u2019s assume that under the tax rules, the company is only able to record $90,000 in revenue, and $65,000 in costs.<\/b> The taxable income is therefore $25,000. The tax payable at a 30% average tax rate is $7,500. This is the actual tax to be paid to the tax authorities for the current period.<\/b> <\/p>\n\n\n\n

    Deferred Tax Assets and Deferred Tax Liabilities<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

    Now, to reconcile the discrepancies between the taxes payable, which is the amount of taxes actually paid, and the income tax expense, which is reflected in the income statement, the differences are recorded in the balance sheet as deferred tax assets and deferred tax liabilities. For our example, the tax expense is $12,000, but taxes payable is $7,500. The company has to record a DTL of $4,500 on its balance sheet to reconcile this discrepancy.<\/p>\n\n\n\n
