Exploring Capital Structure Theories | CFA Level I Corporate Issuers<\/h1>\n\n\n\n

Today, we’ll delve into the world of capital structure decisions by understanding three key theories: MM 1958, MM 1963, and the static trade-off theory.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

Understanding Capital Structure Theories<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

We’ll be discussing three essential theories:<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    \n
  • MM 1958:<\/b> Assumes no taxes and no costs of financial distress.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
  • MM 1963:<\/b> Considers taxes but not costs of financial distress.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
  • Static Trade-Off Theory:<\/b> Takes into account both taxes and costs of financial distress.<\/li>\n<\/ul>\n\n\n\n

    You might be wondering why we bother with MM theories if they don’t perfectly align with reality. Don’t worry; it’ll all become clear soon. For now, remember that MM theories lay the groundwork for understanding the relationship between capital structure, taxes, and the cost of capital<\/strong>. This knowledge is crucial for comprehending the static trade-off theory, which offers a realistic explanation for the link between capital structure and firm value.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    MM Proposition with No Taxes (MM 1958)<\/h2>\n\n\n\n

    Published in 1958 by Professors Modigliani and Miller (hence the MM acronym), this theory features two propositions:<\/p>\n\n\n\n

    Proposition 1: Capital Structure is Irrelevant<\/h3>\n\n\n\n

    In a perfect world with no taxes, capital structure doesn’t matter. This perfect world assumes:<\/p>\n\n\n\n

      \n
    • Perfectly competitive capital markets with no transaction costs, taxes, or bankruptcy costs.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
    • Homogeneous investor expectations.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
    • Riskless borrowing and lending.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
    • No agency costs.<\/li>\n\n\n\n
    • Investment decisions are unaffected by financing decisions.<\/li>\n<\/ul>\n\n\n\n

      Given these assumptions, MM argues that a firm’s value remains the same<\/strong>, regardless of capital structure.<\/p>\n\n\n\n

      Proposition 2: Cost of Capital and Degree of Leverage<\/h3>\n\n\n\n

      Under the same perfect market assumptions, MM explains that the cost of debt must be lower than the cost of equity. However, as a company increases its debt usage, equityholders’ risk rises, which in turn increases the cost of equity. According to MM, the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) remains constant<\/strong> as leverage increases.<\/p>\n\n\n\n
